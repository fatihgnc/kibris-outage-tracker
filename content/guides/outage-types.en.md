---
title: The difference between planned, rotating and fault outages
summary: Three kinds of outage, why they behave differently, and what to expect from each.
updated: 2026-08-23
---

Guessing when the power will come back is mostly a matter of knowing what kind
of outage you are in. Every card on this site carries a label: **PLANNED**,
**ROTATING**, or **FAULT**. The difference is not just wording — the three
happen for different reasons and end in different ways.

## Planned outage

The power is switched off deliberately, and announced in advance, so that
maintenance, repair or renewal work can be done on the network. Replacing a
pole, servicing a transformer, or bringing a new line into use all fall into
this category.

**How you recognise it:** it is announced days or hours ahead. The announcement
gives a date, a time range, and the settlements affected.

**What to expect:** the announced range is a reasonable estimate, because the
crew goes out knowing what the job is. It is still not a guarantee. If the work
finishes early, the power comes back early. If something unexpected turns up, it
runs long.

Planned outages usually last **two to six hours** and tend to start in working
hours, often around 09:00. That is not a coincidence: working in daylight is
both safer and faster.

**What you can do:** this is the one predictable kind. Do the laundry
beforehand, charge what needs charging, and keep the fridge shut.

## Rotating outage

This happens when the electricity being generated does not cover what everyone
is trying to use. To stop the network collapsing entirely, the load is cut from
different areas in turn — one area goes dark for a while, then gets its power
back as another is cut.

**How you recognise it:** the announcement mentions rotation, load shedding, or
a shortfall in generation. It usually covers several areas on the same day.

**What to expect:** shorter than planned work, typically **one to four hours**.
But it can repeat. If the underlying cause has not gone away — a generating unit
out of service, or demand staying high — it may happen again the next day.

These are more likely on hot summer days, at the hours when air conditioning
peaks.

**Why it gets its own label:** a rotating cut is worse news for you than planned
work. Planned maintenance happens once and is done; a rotating cut is a symptom
of a supply problem and may well come back. That is why it does not look the
same as planned work on this site.

## Fault

An unexpected failure: a broken cable, a failed transformer, a vehicle hitting a
pole, lightning, a storm, sometimes an animal.

**How you recognise it:** it is not announced, because by definition nobody knew
it was coming. By the time you notice, it has already started.

**What to expect:** the end time is usually **unknown**. On this site, records
like that show the start time and then "unknown" instead of a range. That is not
a gap in our data — it is honesty. Until a crew finds the fault, nobody knows
how long it will take.

A simple blown fuse might be fixed in half an hour. A pole brought down in a
storm can take a day to replace.

**What you should do:** report it. How to do that is in our
[fault reporting guide](/en/guides/report-a-fault).

## Side by side

| | Planned | Rotating | Fault |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Announced ahead | Yes | Usually | No |
| Typical length | 2–6 hours | 1–4 hours | Unknown |
| End time known | Yes, estimated | Yes, estimated | Usually not |
| Repeats | No | It can | Rarely |
| Cause | Maintenance, renewal | Shortfall in supply | Unexpected failure |

## Why an announced time is never a guarantee

Every duration on this site is an estimate and is labelled as one, for three
reasons.

First, the announcement is written before the work happens. Once the crew is on
site, things can turn out better or worse than expected.

Second, work gets cancelled or postponed. The weather changes, or a crew is
pulled onto something urgent.

Third, we are the side that compiles the announcements, not the side that does
the work. We collect the information automatically from official announcements
and news sources. If something changes at the source, we can only see it once it
is published.

That is why every card links to its source. The definitive answer is always
KIB-TEK's own announcement.

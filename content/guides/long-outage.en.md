A couple of hours without power is an inconvenience for most households. The
longer it runs, the more three things start to matter, usually in this order:
food, water, and anything medical that needs electricity.

## Fridge and freezer

The single most important rule: **keep the door shut.** A closed fridge holds
its temperature for about **four hours**. A full freezer holds for roughly
**48 hours**, a half-full one for about **24**. Every time you open the door,
those numbers get shorter.

If your freezer is mostly empty, filling the space with bottles of water helps:
the bigger the frozen mass, the longer the cold lasts.

When the power returns, do not rely on smell or appearance to decide what to
throw away. The bacteria that cause food poisoning do not necessarily change how
food looks, smells, or tastes. Temperature is what matters.

**Throw away:** meat, chicken, fish, milk, yoghurt, soft cheese, egg dishes,
cooked leftovers, and opened tins — if they have been above fridge temperature
for **more than four hours**.

**Usually fine:** unopened tins, fresh fruit and vegetables, butter and
margarine, hard cheeses, jam, pickles, bread. These are things that keep at room
temperature anyway.

**Frozen food:** if it still has ice crystals and feels cold, it can be
refrozen. If it has fully thawed and gone warm, throw it out. Meat that thawed
and refroze may look fine, but it is not worth the risk.

When in doubt, throw it out. A pot of food costs less than food poisoning.

## Water

Many homes in Northern Cyprus draw water from a tank using an **electric pump**.
When the power goes, whatever is already in the roof tank keeps flowing under
gravity, but the pump cannot refill it.

In practice that means: when the outage starts, you have as much water as your
tank holds, and when that runs out the tap goes dry.

If it looks like the outage will be a long one, fill some containers early. Keep
drinking water separate from water for cleaning and the toilet. A bucket of
spare water is enough to flush once.

When the power comes back, the first water through may run cloudy. Sediment from
the bottom of the tank can get stirred up; running it for a few minutes usually
clears it.

## Medical devices

This is the most serious item on the list, and it needs planning **before** an
outage, not during one.

If someone in the house uses an oxygen concentrator, a CPAP machine, a medicine
pump, an electric wheelchair, or medication that must be refrigerated — insulin,
for example — an outage is more than an inconvenience.

Worth doing now:

- Find out your device's **backup power** situation. Some have an internal
  battery, some do not. Know how long it lasts.
- Keep a **cool bag and ice packs** for refrigerated medication. For insulin,
  check the manufacturer's guidance on how long it may be kept at room
  temperature; most products tolerate a period out of the fridge, but it varies
  by product and should be confirmed from the leaflet.
- If the device is critical, **talk to your doctor or pharmacist** in advance
  about where to go during a long outage.
- Keep your phone and a **power bank** charged.

This site is not the right place for advice on any of that — your medical team
is. The point here is only that it should be thought about before the power
goes, not after.

## Staying cool in summer

In the summer months, losing air conditioning can be the hardest part of a long
outage.

Close shutters and curtains during the day; keeping the sun out works far better
than trying to cool a room afterwards. The coolest part of the house is usually
the ground floor and any north-facing room.

Drink plenty of water rather than waiting until you feel thirsty. Older people
and small children are affected by heat sooner, so check on them through the
day.

Once it is dark and cooler outside than in, open the windows.

## Light and power

A torch is safer than a candle. A candle knocked over is a genuine cause of
fires during outages, so if there are children or pets in the house, use a
torch.

Charge your phone early. If an outage has been announced, top up your power bank
before it starts. You can charge from a car, but **never run the engine in a
closed garage** to do it.

## Generators

If you use a generator, two rules are not negotiable.

**Never run it indoors.** That includes a garage, a balcony, a basement, or
right outside a window. The carbon monoxide in the exhaust is colourless and
has no smell; this is how people die. A generator belongs outdoors, well away
from doors and windows.

**Never wire it straight into the house circuits.** Without a proper transfer
switch, a connection like that can feed electricity back into the network and
put the crews working on it in danger. That is a job for a qualified
electrician.
